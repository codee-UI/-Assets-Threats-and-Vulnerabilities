## 4. Access-Control Issues

### Issue 1 — Former contractor account was not deactivated

Robert Taylor Jr.'s listed contract end date is **27/12/2019**, but the directory still records recent access and the event log associates the matching IP address with a payroll event in 2023.

**Why this is a problem:** Accounts that remain active after employment or contract termination can be used by former personnel or by anyone who compromises their credentials. This violates the principle that access should exist only while there is a valid business need.

### Issue 2 — Excessive administrator privileges

Robert Taylor Jr. is identified as a **legal attorney**, but the directory lists his authorization level as **Admin**. The event is payroll-related, so the recorded account context appears to have had access capable of affecting a sensitive business process.

**Why this is a problem:** Administrator permissions can allow changes beyond a user's job responsibilities. Giving broad access to many employees increases the impact of stolen credentials, mistakes, and misuse. The directory also lists several other employees as Admin, suggesting that privileged access may be assigned too broadly; each assignment should be reviewed rather than assumed necessary.

## 5. Recommended Mitigations

### Recommendation 1 — Enforce account lifecycle management

- Disable accounts promptly when an employee leaves or a contractor's agreement expires.
- Set an account expiration date linked to the contract end date; use automated reminders before expiry and automatic disablement at expiry.
- Require HR or the contract owner to notify IT of starts, role changes, extensions, and departures.
- Review inactive and expired accounts on a regular schedule and remove unnecessary access.
- Preserve logs and records when disabling an account; do not delete evidence needed for an investigation.

**Expected benefit:** Prevents former personnel from retaining access after their business relationship ends.

### Recommendation 2 — Apply least privilege and role-based access control

- Remove unnecessary administrator rights from ordinary employee and contractor accounts.
- Give each person only the access required for their assigned duties.
- Separate legal, payroll-entry, payroll-approval, and system-administration roles.
- Require a second authorized person to approve changes to bank-account or payment details.
- Review privileged accounts periodically and document who approved each exception.

**Expected benefit:** Limits the damage that can be done by a compromised or misused account and reduces the chance that one account can create and approve a fraudulent payment.

### Additional high-value safeguards

- **Enable multifactor authentication (MFA)** for all accounts, especially administrators and users who can access payroll or financial systems.
- **Use unique individual accounts.** Avoid shared administrator credentials; ensure logs identify the individual account that performed each action.
- **Alert on high-risk events:** changes to bank details, payroll destination changes, access by expired accounts, unusual login locations/devices, and administrator activity outside normal patterns.
- **Verify payment changes out of band:** confirm new or changed beneficiary details through a trusted, previously known contact channel—not the contact information included in the change request.
- **Retain and review audit logs:** collect authentication, endpoint, cloud-drive, and payroll records in a central location with restricted access and reliable timestamps.
- **Conduct periodic access reviews:** have managers and system owners certify that each user's permissions remain necessary.

## 6. Authentication vs. Authorization

| Control area | Meaning | Application to this incident |
|---|---|---|
| Authentication | Verifies who is attempting to sign in. | Require MFA, unique user identities, strong credentials, and review of sign-in/device records. |
| Authorization | Determines what an authenticated account is allowed to do. | Disable expired accounts and remove broad Admin privileges; restrict payroll and bank-detail changes to authorized roles. |
| Accountability / auditing | Records actions so they can be investigated. | Preserve logs that link an action to a unique account, device, time, and relevant transaction. |

MFA can reduce the risk of unauthorized sign-ins, but **MFA alone does not correct excessive permissions or an account left active after a contract ends**. Account lifecycle management and least privilege are also necessary.

## 7. Immediate Investigation and Response

Because the payment was reportedly stopped, the organization should still determine whether any systems or records were altered.

1. **Secure the account:** Temporarily disable or restrict the implicated account while preserving evidence. Revoke active sessions and credentials if compromise is suspected.
2. **Validate the event:** Confirm the date format, time zone, event details, and meaning of `FAUX_BANK` in the original payroll system.
3. **Correlate records:** Review identity-provider sign-in logs, MFA records, endpoint/device logs for `Up2-NoGud`, cloud-drive audit logs, and payroll/bank-change history around the event time.
4. **Check the transaction:** Confirm the intended beneficiary, destination account, amount, approval history, and whether any related changes or attempted payments occurred.
5. **Establish attribution carefully:** Interview relevant staff and verify whether the account was shared, compromised, or accessed remotely. Do not conclude that the named employee personally caused the incident until corroborating evidence supports it.
6. **Review similar exposure:** Search for other expired contractors, dormant accounts, broad Admin permissions, and recent changes to payment details.
7. **Document and remediate:** Record the timeline, evidence, decisions, corrective actions, and owners. Follow the organization's incident-response and reporting requirements.
